In Praise of Slow Reading

By Mira Okafor

There is a particular kind of attention that only arrives after the first ten minutes with a book. The restlessness fades, the sentences begin to carry weight, and the room around you recedes. We rarely give ourselves the chance to reach it, because the first ten minutes are exactly when a notification offers an exit.

What speed takes away

Skimming is a useful skill and an honest one. We skim timetables, menus and terms of service, and nothing is lost. The trouble starts when skimming becomes the default mode for everything, including the essays and chapters that were written to be inhabited rather than scanned. A well-built paragraph has a rhythm, and rhythm cannot be skimmed; it can only be heard.

A book is a slow machine for changing a mind, and it only runs at its own speed.

Writers know this. They revise a sentence until its stresses fall in the right places, then readers glide past it at three hundred words a minute and wonder why nothing stuck. The mismatch is nobody's fault, but it is worth noticing.

Small practices

None of this requires a monastery. Read one thing a day without a second window open. Keep a pencil nearby and mark the sentence that made you pause. At the end of a chapter, close the book and say, out loud, what it argued. If you cannot, you have found the exact place to reread.

Slow reading is not about reading less. It is about letting a few things finish arriving before the next thing begins. The reward is not efficiency. It is the quiet pleasure of having actually been somewhere.
